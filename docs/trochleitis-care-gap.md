# Trochleitis and barriers to care

Recognition diagnosis and access to treatment

Trochleitis is a recognized cause of pain around the eye, yet the clinical literature repeatedly describes difficulty recognizing and treating it. The problem begins when a potentially treatable source of pain is missed, confused with another headache disorder, or left without an effective management plan. Better awareness matters because a diagnosis that remains unfamiliar can leave patients facing prolonged symptoms before appropriate treatment is considered. [1-3]

## A small anatomical structure can cause substantial pain

The trochlea is a small pulley near the upper inner corner of the eye socket, through which the superior oblique tendon passes. Trochleitis refers to inflammation of this region. Trochleodynia is a broader term for pain arising there, including disorders without demonstrable inflammation. The International Classification of Headache Disorders recognizes trochlear headache associated with inflammation or dysfunction. Pain can worsen with eye movement or reading, and trochleitis can also trigger migraine. [1]

Persistent pain in this area deserves assessment, including consideration of other causes. Taking the problem seriously includes explaining what is suspected, what has been excluded and what happens next.

## Under recognition is documented in clinical research

In a Mayo Clinic cohort of 25 patients with trochlear headache, the referring neurologist or ophthalmologist had not recognized the diagnosis in any patient. Published in 2014, the study included inflammatory and primary trochlear headache. This selected specialist cohort cannot establish a population-wide rate, but it demonstrates that the diagnosis can escape recognition among clinicians working with eye and headache complaints. [3]

A 2004 series of 18 people with primary trochlear headache reported that all had previously received other headache diagnoses and treatment. Seventy percent had experienced pain for more than a year. This non-inflammatory pain group demonstrates the consequences of overlooking the trochlear region, although it was not a cohort of inflammatory trochleitis. [4]

A 2023 report describes an 18-year-old whose trochleitis was initially treated as migraine for more than two months. The authors reported successful local steroid treatment after insufficient improvement with anti-inflammatory medication. This illustrates delay in inflammatory trochleitis itself, without establishing its frequency. [5]

## Normal imaging can leave important questions unanswered

A 2019 review describes trochleodynia as predominantly a clinical diagnosis: only 20% of 115 pooled patients had CT or MRI abnormalities. Normal imaging therefore does not exclude trochleodynia. This concerns broader trochlear pain, not proof of inflammatory trochleitis in everyone with eye pain and a normal scan. [2]

A reassuring scan needs to be interpreted alongside the history and examination. Persistent symptoms still require an explanation and a plan.

## Recognition and treatment access are separate problems

A diagnosis only helps if it leads to an appropriate care pathway. A 2024 literature review identifies limited familiarity, low prevalence and a lack of shared diagnostic and treatment guidelines as contributors to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. It also describes disagreement about the sequence of treatments. Those findings help explain why clinicians may approach the same condition differently. They do not measure how often patients are refused a procedure or encounter dismissive behaviour. [6]

Recognition needs to lead to a workable care pathway. If a clinician suspects trochleitis but does not provide the relevant assessment or treatment, the patient needs a referral destination and follow-up. Without those arrangements, knowing the name of a condition may still leave someone searching for care. This is a service implication drawn from the clinical problem.

## Treatment can help but response is not uniform

Published management discussions include oral anti-inflammatory medication and local corticosteroid injections. Choice depends on the presentation and individual risk. The 2019 review supports considering injections for severe or persistent symptoms, while acknowledging limited evidence and the need for better trials. [2]

In the Mayo cohort, responses to local dexamethasone and lidocaine injections were generally favourable but not universal. Some patients required serial injections; 10 of 25 had complete remission at a median follow-up of 34 months. These results support the possibility of meaningful benefit while showing that one injection does not reliably end every patient's illness. [3]

Safe access requires an informed decision, appropriate procedural expertise and reassessment if benefit is absent or temporary. Deferring an injection should come with an explanation and a plan. Suspected trochleitis does not establish that an injection is appropriate, and declining one does not automatically constitute poor care.

## The burden extends into ordinary daily activities

Reading and eye movement can provoke trochlear headache, potentially affecting study, screen-based work and everyday tasks. This is a practical implication of the symptom pattern, not a quantified employment outcome from these studies. [1]

The need for diagnostic care and the need for symptom support can exist at the same time. People with persistent eye pain need their functional difficulties heard, clear communication about uncertainty, and a defined point for reassessment. Waiting for a definitive explanation should not leave the burden of coordinating care entirely with the person in pain.

## What better care should provide

A reasonable service response would combine recognition of the symptom pattern, appropriate specialist assessment, an explained treatment decision and continuity when the initial approach fails. These are proposals for improving care rather than a treatment algorithm tested by the cited studies.

The evidence is strongest for under-recognition and diagnostic complexity. These publications do not quantify dismissal, waiting times or access barriers worldwide. Better research and clearer pathways are needed. Persistent pain deserves attention, and uncertainty about its cause should lead to a plan the patient can understand and follow.
